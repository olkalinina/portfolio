CROSaaS

Know your product before you
measure it

A practical guide to SaaS analytics, conversion funnels and product health.

When I review SaaS products, I often see the same problem: founders have plenty of questions about conversion, but aren't always sure what they should be looking at. Some believe they can't improve anything without analytics. Others track dozens of metrics but struggle to understand what the numbers actually mean. And A/B testing is often treated as the obvious next step, even when nobody has established what the underlying problem is.

You don't need a sophisticated analytics setup to start evaluating your product. You can learn a lot by going through the experience as a new user, looking at what your website promises, and checking whether the product delivers on that promise. You can also make better use of the data you already have, rather than tracking more things without knowing what to do with them.

This article is for founders who may not have the budget to hire a CRO specialist, or who simply want to understand and improve their product themselves before bringing someone in. It walks through the SaaS journey, from the first website visit to the point where a user gets value from the product, and explains what you can examine at each stage, which metrics are worth tracking, and how to investigate problems even if you have little or no user data.

1. Start with the user journey, not the dashboard

A common trap is staring at single numbers without the journey behind them. You may know how many people visit your site, how many create an account and how many start a subscription. Do you know how many of them ever see the value you promised?

A conversion funnel doesn't end at sign-up. Registration is where the product experience begins. So start by mapping the whole path, from the first touch on your marketing site to the moment someone gets the core value of the product.

The nine stages of a SaaS journey

Not every product follows this path. A self-serve tool can take someone from the website to activation in one sitting. An enterprise platform might need a demo, internal approval, technical setup and several people before anyone sees value. Map the journey your business actually has.

Conversion, activation and value are different things

People mix these up all the time, so here is how I use them.

Conversion is the action that moves someone to the next stage of your business: a completed sign-up, a trial start, a demo request.

Activation is the moment a new user first experiences the core value of the product. It should be a meaningful action. Creating an account doesn't count.

Value outcome is the result the customer is after: a report they can use, a finished project, a manual task that no longer has to be done by hand.

Take an AI analytics platform:

  • Conversion: the user creates an account.
  • Activation: they connect a real data source and generate a report.
  • Value outcome: they use that report to make a business decision.

Creating an account only means someone was curious enough to try the product. It says nothing yet about whether it helped.

How to define activation

Start from the promise your website makes. What outcome are you selling, and what is the first moment inside the product where a user can see that outcome becoming real? Some examples:

Product typeWeak activation signalBetter candidate
Analytics platformUser logs inUser connects a data source and generates a useful report
Project management toolUser creates a projectUser adds tasks and starts managing work with their team
AI productUser sends a promptUser uses or exports an AI-generated result in their real workflow
Collaboration toolUser creates a workspaceSeveral team members start exchanging content
Marketing platformUser installs the productUser publishes a campaign or gets a first meaningful result

Treat these as starting hypotheses. The right activation event is the action that shows people are starting to get value and, ideally, predicts that they will stay. If you have enough data, compare retention for users who did the candidate action with those who didn't. If you have very little, talk to your best customers and ask when they first realized the product was useful.

2. Your website and your product have to tell the same story

The marketing website and the product often get treated as two separate design jobs. For the person on the other side of the screen it is one experience. The website sets expectations and the product has to meet them.

Say your site promises that users can automate their reporting in minutes. A visitor signs up and lands on an empty dashboard. To get anywhere, they have to connect several integrations, set permissions and learn terms they've never seen. The website promised a result and the product handed them a setup project.

That gap can hurt conversion even when the website and the product each look fine on their own.

What your website should answer

Before you dig into analytics, check whether a potential customer could understand your product and make an informed decision. The site should answer a handful of plain questions.

What is this, and who is it for? Say what the product does, who it helps and what problem it solves. A line like "transform your workflow" means little until you explain what changes.

What do I get out of it? Features aren't benefits. "Advanced reporting" tells a visitor almost nothing. Showing how those reports help someone spot revenue opportunities tells them a lot.

How does it work? Screenshots, demos, examples and realistic use cases help people picture what they're signing up for. This matters most for complex and AI-powered products.

Can I trust it? Testimonials, case studies, customers people recognize, security information and honest communication all reduce uncertainty. How much proof is enough depends on the product and on how risky it is to buy.

How much does it cost? Self-serve products usually do better with clear prices and clear differences between plans. Enterprise products with custom contracts need another approach, but even a starting price or an example helps people judge the investment.

What happens next? "Start free trial", "Book a demo" and "Create an account" promise different things. The next screen should deliver what the button said.

A website check you can do without analytics

Show your homepage to someone who has never seen the product. Give them five to ten seconds, then ask:

  • What does this product do?
  • Who is it for?
  • What problem does it solve?
  • What do you expect to happen if you click the main button?

If the answers are fuzzy, you've found your first thing to improve. Do the same with the pricing page and key product pages. Can people tell the plans apart? Do they know what's included? Are their obvious objections answered? You don't need scroll-depth data to know that a pricing page is confusing.

3. Which metrics actually matter

Once the journey is clear, you can decide what to measure. The biggest mistake here is tracking everything just because the tool lets you. Page views, sign-ups, daily active users and button clicks can all be useful, but none of them tells you on its own whether people get value.

A good metric answers a specific question and points to something you can look into or change. For an early-stage SaaS, I'd start with one primary business outcome and a small set of metrics across acquisition, conversion, activation and retention. The right number depends on your product and business model.

Acquisition: are you attracting the right people?

Look at where visitors come from and whether they take the next step.

Traffic by source. Which channels bring people to the site: organic search, paid campaigns, referrals, social, direct.

Visit-to-conversion rate. The share of visitors who complete the next step you want, whether that's a sign-up, a trial start or a demo request.

Conversions ÷ unique visitors × 100

Landing-page conversion by source. Compare how different channels and landing pages perform.

A low conversion rate doesn't automatically mean the site is badly designed. You may be attracting the wrong audience, or your ad may promise something the landing page doesn't. A campaign aimed at students could bring plenty of traffic to a B2B analytics platform and almost no qualified sign-ups. Look at the quality of traffic, not only the volume.

Evaluation and sign-up: can interested people take the next step?

When someone decides to try the product, signing up should be easy.

Sign-up completion rate.

Completed sign-ups ÷ started sign-ups × 100

If many people start registering and don't finish, look at the process. Are you asking for information you don't need yet? Is a credit card required when they didn't expect it? Are there technical errors? Is it clear what happens after registration?

Step-by-step funnel conversion. How many people move from each step to the next. This shows where the biggest drop-off is. It doesn't say why.

Activation: are users getting value?

This is one of the most important areas for a SaaS product.

Activation rate.

Users who reach the activation event within N days ÷ users who signed up in the cohort × 100

Define the activation event carefully. It should be a real milestone, not just any interaction.

Onboarding completion. How many users finish the important setup steps. It shows where onboarding gets hard or where people stop.

Time to first value (TTFV). How long it takes a new user to reach the first meaningful outcome. If your product promises automated analytics, how long does it take someone to connect their data and get a first useful report? Use the median where you can, since a few users with very long setups will skew the average. Track the users who never reach first value at all, too.

Retention: do people come back?

A product that gets sign-ups but doesn't keep users has a basic problem to investigate.

Cohort retention. Group users by when they signed up or activated, then see how many keep doing something meaningful over time. For example: of the people who signed up in the first week of October, what share are still doing the core action in week four?

Repeat value events. Don't only count logins. Look at whether people come back to do things that deliver value. Someone who opens the app every morning isn't necessarily getting anything out of it.

Trial-to-paid or free-to-paid conversion. How many users or accounts move to a paid plan. Read it next to activation. If people who activate convert well but most new users never activate, the problem is probably onboarding rather than pricing.

Segment your numbers

Averages hide differences. Say your SaaS has a 30% activation rate. That looks like one number, but maybe agencies activate at 60% and independent users at 10%. Those groups have different needs, workflows and expectations, and the average won't tell you which experience is working.

Where it makes sense, split your metrics by:

  • Acquisition source
  • Device
  • User role
  • Company size
  • Subscription plan
  • Use case
  • New versus returning users

Metrics show where to look, not why

A funnel might show that 55% of users never connect a data source. That's a useful finding, but it doesn't tell you whether the instructions are unclear, something is failing technically, permissions are missing, the setup was a surprise, or people simply don't see why they should connect their data.

To find the reason you need other evidence: session recordings, user interviews, support requests or watching someone use the product.

4. What if you don't have analytics?

This is where many founders get stuck. They assume that without analytics they can't know what users do or make informed improvements. That's not quite right.

Without data you can't reliably calculate conversion rates or spot statistical patterns. You can still investigate the experience, find usability problems, understand expectations and see what stops people from getting value. If your product is early and you have few users, watching a handful of them is often worth more than weeks spent building dashboards.

Step 1. Map the journey yourself

Write down every step a new user takes, from discovering your site to their first meaningful result in the product:

  • The landing page
  • Pricing and product information
  • Sign-up or demo request
  • Account creation
  • Onboarding
  • Setup and integrations
  • The first key action
  • The first meaningful result

For each step, note what the user needs to understand, decide and do. If you can't say what happens between two steps, start there.

Step 2. Go through the product as a new user

Create a fresh account and use the product without admin rights or the knowledge you have as its creator. Try it on desktop and on mobile. At each screen, ask yourself:

  • Is it obvious what this screen is for?
  • Do I know what to do next?
  • Does this match what the website promised?
  • Am I being asked for information or permissions too early?
  • Would someone new understand the terminology?
  • Do the empty states help?
  • Do error messages explain how to recover?
  • Do I get feedback when an action works?
  • Is there a point where I don't know how to continue?

Pay special attention to empty states. A blank dashboard is normal for a new user, but it should say what to do next and why.

Step 3. Watch real users

Your own run-through helps, but you already know how the product works. New users don't. Ask five or so people from your target audience to complete realistic tasks, for example:

You're in charge of marketing at a small company and want to know which campaigns brought in the most sign-ups last month. Explore this product and try to find out.

Ask them to think aloud as they work. Don't explain the interface and don't nudge them toward the right button. Watch where they hesitate, what they misread and where they get stuck. Afterwards, ask:

  • What did you expect to happen?
  • What was confusing?
  • Was there anything you couldn't find?
  • When did you stop being sure what to do next?
  • Would you use this product for the task you were given? Why, or why not?

Five users can show you a lot of usability problems in a focused flow, but this is qualitative research, not measurement. It doesn't mean that 80% of your users run into the same problem. If you have distinct groups, such as admins and everyday users, test them separately.

Step 4. Read the feedback you already have

Your users may already be pointing at the problems. Go through:

  • Support tickets
  • Live chat conversations
  • Sales-call notes
  • Customer interviews
  • Cancellation reasons
  • Feature requests
  • Onboarding questions

Look for questions that keep coming back. If several people separately ask how to connect an integration, find a report or invite their team, something in the experience probably needs work.

Step 5. Keep findings and assumptions apart

Not everything you find rests on the same amount of evidence. I keep three piles:

TypeWhat it meansExample
Observed problemYou saw a user run into itA user couldn't find the integration settings
Repeated patternThe same issue showed up across several users or sourcesFour users and several support tickets mention confusing integration instructions
HypothesisA plausible explanation nobody has checked yetThe integration page may be hard to find because of the navigation

The split stops you from treating guesses as facts and spending time on problems that may not exist.

How to prioritize what you find

You'll probably find more than you can fix right away. Weigh each issue on four things:

  1. Impact. Does it sit on a critical step of the conversion or activation journey?
  2. Severity. Does it cause confusion, delay, failure or abandonment?
  3. Evidence. Did you see it happen, or is it still a hypothesis?
  4. Effort. How much time and development work would the fix take?

A broken sign-up button is an obvious priority. A small visual inconsistency on an internal settings page probably isn't. If a fix is expensive and rests only on assumptions, collect more evidence before you build it.

5. A minimum viable analytics setup

Once you understand the journey and know what you want to look into, analytics become far more useful. You don't have to track every click or build a heavy reporting system. Start with the questions. For an early-stage self-serve SaaS they might be:

  • Which acquisition channels bring users who actually activate?
  • How many visitors start and finish registration?
  • Where do users stop during onboarding?
  • How long does it take them to reach a first meaningful result?
  • How many activated users come back to do the core action?

Those questions should decide what you track.

Website analytics and product analytics do different jobs

Website analytics, such as Google Analytics 4, show acquisition and behavior on the site: where visitors come from, which pages they view and whether they take the next step.

Product analytics, such as Amplitude or Mixpanel, show what users do inside the application: which actions they complete, where they stop, how they engage and whether they return.

For many SaaS products you need both to see the whole journey. One detail matters a lot: connecting acquisition data with product behavior. If someone arrives from a LinkedIn campaign, signs up and eventually activates, you want to be able to link those events. Otherwise marketing optimizes for sign-ups while the product team optimizes for activation, and nobody notices that some channels bring users who never see the value.

Start with a small event plan

You don't need hundreds of events. For a hypothetical B2B analytics platform, a first event map could look like this:

StageEventWhat it tells you
Websitepage_viewedWhich pages users visit
Websitecta_clickedWhich calls to action get attention
Sign-upsignup_startedHow many users begin registration
Sign-upsignup_completedHow many finish registration
Onboardingworkspace_createdWhether initial setup begins
Onboardingdata_source_connectedWhether users complete a key integration
Onboardingdata_source_connection_failedWhether integration attempts fail
Activationreport_createdWhether users build their first report
Activationreport_sharedWhether users take a meaningful next step
Engagementreport_viewedWhether users return to their reports
Monetizationsubscription_startedWhether users become paying customers

This is an illustration, not a template to copy. Choose events that match the real actions and value of your own product.

For every event, write down exactly when it fires. data_source_connected, for example, should mean the system confirmed a successful connection, not that someone clicked the Connect button. Also decide which properties you'll need, such as acquisition source, user role or subscription plan.

Check that the data can be trusted

Before you make decisions from a dashboard:

  • Test the important events yourself.
  • Check that they fire at the right moment.
  • Make sure nothing is counted twice.
  • Keep event names and definitions consistent.
  • Compare critical events, such as completed sign-ups or payments, with your own database where you can.

Bad tracking leads to confident but wrong decisions. Also collect only the data you need, handle personal information responsibly and put proper privacy and consent controls in place for your users.

6. A/B testing is not a magic fix

A/B testing is often treated as the answer to every conversion problem. Something isn't converting? Test another headline. Sign-ups are low? Test another button. Users aren't activating? Test another onboarding screen.

But an A/B test doesn't diagnose anything. It compares two versions of an experience and tells you whether one did better on a chosen metric, under the conditions of that experiment. It can't tell you why people behave the way they do, and it doesn't come up with good ideas for you.

Why it may not suit your product

A test needs enough users and conversions to tell a real difference from random variation. Say your landing page gets 500 visitors a month and converts 3% of them. That's about 15 conversions a month. Split the traffic between two versions and you probably won't collect enough to spot a small improvement with any confidence. The smaller the effect you're after, the more data you need.

That's why testing a button color or a minor headline change on a low-traffic site is usually a poor use of time.

What to do instead

Start with the evidence you already have. If users keep misunderstanding your value proposition, make it clearer. If several of them can't finish onboarding because an integration is confusing, look into that step and improve it. If the sign-up form has fields you don't need, think about removing them or asking later. None of these needs an A/B test first.

When a test does make sense

  • You have a clearly defined conversion or product metric.
  • You have enough traffic and conversions for the test.
  • You have a specific hypothesis backed by evidence.
  • You know what you're changing and why.
  • You can measure the result without misleading side effects.

A good hypothesis might read like this:

Users hesitate to start a free trial because they don't know whether a credit card is required. We believe a clear "No credit card required" message next to the button will increase completed sign-ups without reducing activation.

That idea comes from an observed problem, not from someone guessing which button color looks better.

What if there is no clear winner?

An inconclusive result isn't wasted effort. It may mean the change had little effect, the effect was too small to detect, or the hypothesis was wrong. A negative result can also save you from shipping something that makes the experience worse. The point of experimenting is to learn, not to collect a string of winners.

7. Turn findings into improvements

You don't need an analytics department to start making better product decisions. You need a repeatable way to connect what you see with what you change. A simple loop is enough:

A few examples of how it plays out:

What you noticeWhat to look intoPossible next step
Visitors arrive but few sign upAudience quality, value proposition, pricing, trust, what the button promisesMake the message clearer or remove friction from the decision
Users sign up but don't activateOnboarding, setup requirements, empty states, time to first valueShorten the path to the first meaningful action
Users activate but don't come backWhether the product gives recurring value and fits their workflowLook at retention patterns and why people leave
The same support question keeps coming upNavigation, terminology, missing instructions, unclear functionalityImprove the interface or add guidance where it's needed
Analytics show a big drop-off but no explanationSession recordings, interviews, support feedbackStudy that specific step before deciding what to change

What matters is not jumping straight from a metric to a solution. A drop in activation doesn't automatically mean you should redesign onboarding. It means you need to find out what keeps people from reaching activation. Sometimes it's the interface. Sometimes it's the wrong audience, an expectation the website set too high, a missing integration, or an activation event that was defined badly. Let the evidence decide the next step.

8. An action plan for founders

If you don't know where to start, go in this order.

Understand.

  1. Name your business model: self-serve, sales-led, freemium, free trial or a mix.
  2. Map the journey from the first website visit to the first meaningful result in the product.
  3. Define what activation means for your product.
  4. Go through the whole experience as a new user, on desktop and mobile.

Observe.

  1. Ask a few target users to complete realistic tasks.
  2. Read customer feedback, support requests and sales conversations.
  3. Write down observed problems, repeated patterns and unchecked hypotheses.
  4. Prioritize the issues that affect the journey most.

Measure.

  1. Pick the most important conversion, activation and retention events.
  2. Set up a small tracking plan.
  3. Check that the data is accurate.
  4. Build a few reports that answer specific business questions.

Improve.

  1. Dig into the biggest friction point.
  2. Form a hypothesis based on evidence.
  3. Make a targeted improvement.
  4. See what changed and keep learning.

You don't have to finish all of this before your first improvement. If you find a broken sign-up form or an onboarding step that keeps confusing people, fix it. The aim is to make decisions on purpose instead of on assumptions.

Conclusion: understand first, then measure

Analytics are valuable, but they don't replace understanding your product. A dashboard can show that users leave during onboarding. It can't always tell you whether they were confused, overwhelmed, missing an integration or just the wrong audience. A/B testing can compare two solutions, but it can't tell you which problem is worth solving. And a product with very little traffic can still have obvious problems that stop people from seeing its value.

So start by mapping the journey. Check what your website promises and whether the product delivers it. Watch how people use it. Pick a few meaningful metrics and use analytics to answer the questions that matter. You don't need to measure everything, only to understand enough to make the next decision well.
