Skeuomorphism in design: definition, application, and relevance

Skeuomorphism is a design practice that incorporates elements of the real world into digital interfaces to create a sense of recognition. The term ‘skeuomorph’ refers to an object or design that imitates the characteristics of something else, essentially a copy or derivative. In other words, it’s the transfer of meaning, functions, and properties of a real object to a virtual one.

When computers and the internet became commonplace, skeuomorphic elements played a crucial role: using metaphors and analogies from familiar physical objects as inspiration for interface elements such as icons (trash can, folder, document, home, etc.). This allowed users who were new to digital products to intuitively understand the purpose and functionality of these tools by associating them with already familiar objects.

Skeuomorphism as a visual design trend

In the late 2000s and early 2010s, skeuomorphism evolved from simply using physical metaphors for functionality into a major visual design trend. The interfaces of that period strived to reproduce not only the purpose but the entire outward appearance and sensory experience of their physical counterparts as accurately as possible.

The visual language of the time was characterized by pronounced realism with excessive use of realistic textures, shadows, gradients, and 3D effects to create a tangible, three-dimensional aesthetic imitating physical objects such as wood, leather, and paper. E-book readers like iBooks were designed to look like physical bookshelves with virtual books. Calendar apps resembled actual desktop calendars.

The goal was to create digital interfaces that looked and felt as visually similar to their real-world analogues as possible. While initially making unfamiliar interfaces feel more grounded and lifelike, the excessive details also led to visual element overload, processor strain, and slower load times.

Despite its subsequent overuse, skeuomorphism in design played an important role in developing initial digital literacy among users. By using metaphors of familiar objects as instructional aids, skeuomorphism made new technologies more understandable and familiar to fledgling users.

Criticism and decline

As the novelty of skeuomorphism wore off and digital interfaces became more prevalent, critics began pointing out the limitations of this design approach. Key criticisms included:

  • Difficulty scaling skeuomorphic designs to different screen sizes and device types due to proportions based on physical objects
  • Visual clutter from excessive decorative textures and effects
  • Slower load times and processing needs due to graphical complexity
  • Apps and widgets were becoming increasingly complex and multi-functional, whereas physical objects were often limited in features and purpose
  • An outdated, inauthentic aesthetic look as it became excessive

The backlash against skeuomorphism’s constraints and excesses led to the rise of flat design and minimalist approaches distancing themselves from physical metaphors. As users became more technologically experienced, the need for such “hand-holding” through skeuomorphic realism diminished.

The resurgence of skeuomorphism

Although skeuomorphism fell out of fashion, some designers argue it could reemerge for certain use cases and audiences who struggle with adopting digital technologies and abstractions. For example, research by Ellis and Marshall explored using skeuomorphism to create more intuitive online banking interfaces for the elderly.

Their findings showed that while elderly users preferred the clean aesthetic of flat design visually, they still benefited significantly from skeuomorphic cues and metaphors when it came to learning and understanding the interface’s functionality. Realistic textures like shadows or paper edges provided helpful cues to these users about what actions would occur.

Thus, skeuomorphism may play an important role in ensuring initial accessibility for new technologies before transitioning to more abstract interfaces as users become adept with new tech.

Skeuomorphism now

Researchers in human-computer interaction speculate that skeuomorphism could reemerge as a methodology for easing the introduction of radically new interfaces and technologies like virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and wearable devices (smart watches, glasses, etc.).

For instance, while VR unlocks countless possibilities for designing entirely novel virtual spaces and objects, skeuomorphism could be a useful tool initially for “grounding” unfamiliar new solutions in familiar physical objects. Modeling virtual objects based on real-world counterparts will make them more comprehensible.

Smartwatch interfaces today still largely mimic classic analog watches — this was an intentional design choice to make the new device format relatable and familiar before transitioning to more optimized interfaces tailored specifically for wearables.

The cyclical nature of design

Design trends are cyclical — as user needs evolve alongside shifting technological and cultural contexts, certain skeuomorphic traits may reappear in interfaces blending innovation and recognizability.

While the overt skeuomorphic realism of the early 2010s fell out of fashion, the core principle of skeuomorphism using physical metaphors to ease the introduction of new technologies remains relevant, especially for onboarding radically new interfaces. As we evolve, skeuomorphism will likely continually resurface in new forms adapted to contemporary design constraints and user needs.
