CRO audit vs. UX audit: what’s the difference?

Both start with many of the same tools. What separates them is the question they’re built around, and that decides what you get back.

The short answer

A CRO audit and a UX audit use mostly the same tools. Heuristic review, analytics, session recordings, surveys, user testing: either one can include any of them. What differs is the question the work is built around.

A CRO audit starts from a business event, like a signup, a trial turning into a paid plan, or a demo request, and asks why people who should complete it don’t. A UX audit starts from the person using the product and asks whether they can get what they came for without effort or confusion.

The two approaches often overlap, and the terms are not always used consistently. Their findings can also overlap, but the emphasis tends to differ: a CRO audit connects potential barriers to a defined business outcome, while a UX audit examines how effectively people can complete tasks and achieve their goals. In either case, the quality of the findings depends on the evidence behind them.

When choosing an audit, the label matters less than the questions it will investigate, the evidence it will use, and how its findings will inform decisions. That is the focus of this article.

Why the terminology varies

Neither term has a standard definition. Definitions vary across agencies and research firms. Some describe UX evaluation as one component of a broader CRO process; others position UX auditing as the primary diagnostic step before testing conversion-related changes. These differences reflect how individual providers define and package their services, rather than a universally agreed distinction between the two disciplines.

In practice, “CRO audit” can mean any of three things:

  • a review of your pages against a list of best practices,
  • an analytics investigation of where your funnel leaks, or
  • a research project that combines both with customer evidence and ends in a testing plan.

“UX audit” stretches just as far, from one expert looking at a handful of screens to a mixed-methods study with real users. Prices stretch with it. The public price lists I found run from low four figures to well into five.

So when a vendor says “audit,” the useful question isn’t which kind. It’s what they’ll actually look at, and how you’ll know that what they found is true.

The warning: this is a tendency, not a rule. A good UX audit can use analytics and tie every finding to a business goal. A good CRO audit can include proper usability testing. “CRO is numbers, UX is feelings” is a common shorthand, and it is too simple to be useful.

For reference, the standard definition of usability (ISO 9241-11) is about effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction for specific users doing specific tasks. That’s the lens a UX audit is built on. A CRO audit adds one more: does this help the business get the outcome it’s measuring?

Where UX and conversion meet, and where they don’t

Usability problems do cost conversions. The best-studied case is e-commerce checkout. Baymard Institute has tracked cart abandonment for more than a decade, and the global average sits near 70%, calculated from roughly fifty published studies. Baymard also estimates that the average large store could lift conversion by about 35% through checkout design alone.

70%average cart abandonment, averaged across about 50 studies (Baymard)
42%of US shoppers in Baymard’s survey abandoned a cart because they were “just browsing”
2× to ~4×more sign-ups with mild and aggressive dark patterns (Luguri & Strahilevitz)

These figures need to be interpreted in context. Baymard’s estimate of potential conversion improvement is a modeled estimate based on checkout research, not a result that can be assumed for every business. Its survey also found that some shoppers abandon carts because they are browsing or are not ready to buy. This distinction matters beyond e-commerce: not every visitor who leaves a SaaS website has encountered a UX problem. Some are researching alternatives, evaluating pricing, or simply not ready to purchase. Improving the interface will not necessarily address these issues.

It runs the other way too. A perfectly usable interface can convert badly because the offer is unclear, the price is off, or the traffic is wrong. And a product can convert well while treating people badly.

Luguri and Strahilevitz ran randomized experiments on representative samples of US adults, offering what was in fact a dubious identity-theft-protection service inside a survey. Mild dark patterns more than doubled the share who signed up. Aggressive ones nearly quadrupled it, and also produced a strong backlash. Conversion went up. Nobody would call that a good experience. It was a survey scenario, not a SaaS product, but it makes the point: a metric moving doesn’t prove people were helped, and a team that optimizes one number can find that the wrong thing works.

A symptom is not a cause

Analytics can show where users drop off and how frequently it happens. On its own, however, it rarely explains the reasons behind that behavior. Understanding the cause usually requires additional evidence, such as session recordings, surveys, interviews, or usability testing. Without that evidence, teams risk treating an observed symptom as an established cause.

Here’s a case from my own work. A European store selling made-to-measure products had a funnel that fell apart at the configurator, the step where the buyer enters dimensions and shape. A standard checklist would likely have said what checklists say: add measurement guidance, add a sizing guide.

When I went through the site, the guidance already existed. It was good, with worked examples, a note on measuring the finished item rather than the thing it covers, and instructions for unusual shapes. It was sitting behind small pale info icons, a secondary tab, and a footer link.

The buyer’s real fear at that moment is “can I trust myself to measure this right?” A wrong order can’t be returned. The reassurance was in the building. It just wasn’t where they were standing.

Two more findings pointed the same way. Free samples and the wishlist, the tools that help an unsure buyer, both required an account. And the checkout was fine. I said so in the report, because knowing where the problem isn’t saves a client money.

The recommendation was to move the existing text into the configurator rather than write anything new. It’s worth being clear about what that diagnosis rests on: a close read of the site plus input from outside observers. It’s a well-grounded expert hypothesis. It hasn’t been tested yet, and I’d say so to the client.

Checklist, expert review, diagnosis

Think of these as rungs on a ladder. Each does a different job.

A checklist tells you whether common patterns are present or missing. It can’t tell you whether they matter for your product.

An expert review gives you plausible problems with reasons attached. It’s quick, cheap, and good at catching obvious defects. But it depends on who does it. Hertzum and Jacobsen reviewed the research on what’s called the evaluator effect and found that the average agreement between two evaluators examining the same system with the same method ranged from 5% to 65%. In one study, four evaluators analyzed the same four recorded usability sessions and logged 93 distinct problems between them. Only 20% were found by all four. Almost half, 46%, were found by one person only.

This does not make expert review unhelpful. It means that findings from a single reviewer should be treated as informed judgments rather than a complete or definitive account of usability problems. Confidence can be strengthened by involving additional evaluators, comparing findings with behavioral data, or observing real users performing relevant tasks. Hertzum and Jacobsen’s own advice is simply to involve more than one evaluator.

Analytics helps establish where a problem occurs and how widespread it is. Research with users can help explain why it occurs. A sound diagnosis combines relevant evidence and makes clear which conclusions are well supported, which remain hypotheses, and what should be investigated next.

The chain I use for every finding

I use this six-step sequence to structure findings in my reports. The individual practices are established elements of diagnostic and research work; the way I combine them into this sequence is my own methodology, not a formal industry standard.

Here it is with a real finding, anonymized, from a property marketplace:

  1. ObservationVisitors arriving directly, the biggest traffic group, engaged about 10% of the time. Visitors from organic search engaged about 73% of the time.
  2. EvidenceThe homepage showed two neighboring cards: zero commission for sellers, 1% commission for buyers, with nothing saying who each applied to. Saving a listing or making an offer also opened a registration popup straight away.
  3. InterpretationA first-time visitor can’t tell what the platform charges, and the page asks “how do I search?” before “why should I?” This is a hypothesis. Direct traffic is a mixed bucket in analytics, so I wouldn’t call it proven.
  4. Potential impactIt sits in front of every visitor and the largest traffic group, so it comes first by reach.
  5. RecommendationLabel each card by audience, show the 1% next to what buyers usually pay, and add a short “how it works.” Let people browse and save listings before asking them to register.
  6. ValidationA new visitor can say who pays what. Direct-traffic engagement rises over a two-week window. I set 30% as the target. That’s my threshold, not an industry benchmark.

Some problems aren’t audit problems

The property marketplace is a good example. Its biggest constraint wasn’t on the page at all: almost no owners had listed a property, so a prospective seller had no proof the model worked. No copy change fixes that. It takes recruiting the first listings by hand. In the report I split the roadmap in two and said plainly that the last three items were “not primarily CRO problems.”

The other example is a B2B software-services vendor. Its homepage hero spoke to enterprise buyers, and the section right below spoke to startups and small businesses. The strongest proof, its ratings and partner status, was buried deep in the site, and published case studies still had placeholder text in them. A UX audit would have cleaned up those pages. The underlying issue was upstream of the interface: the company had not clearly defined its primary buyer. That is a positioning decision the company needs to make; a UX or CRO audit can surface the consequences, but cannot make that strategic choice on its behalf.

Where A/B testing fits

A/B tests evaluate specific changes; they do not, by themselves, explain why the original experience underperforms. A useful sequence is to investigate the problem, form a hypothesis, and test the proposed change when the available traffic and experimental conditions make a reliable result feasible. Testing without a clear hypothesis risks spending time on changes that do not address the underlying problem.

Feasibility matters here. A common rule of thumb, popularized by Ron Kohavi, who led experimentation at Microsoft, puts the sample you need at about 16 × variance ÷ (smallest effect you want to detect)², per variant, for 80% power at the usual 5% significance level.

Plug in a 5% baseline conversion rate and a relative 5% lift, from 5.00% to 5.25%. That comes to roughly 120,000 visitors per variant. At a 2% baseline it’s about 310,000. That’s my arithmetic from the formula, so treat the figures as an order of magnitude. Still, many B2B funnels never see that kind of volume.

When you can’t test, there are options:

  • Test bigger changes. Large effects need smaller samples.
  • Test higher in the funnel, where the traffic is.
  • Run usability sessions on the variants and watch real people use them.
  • Release in stages and compare cohorts before and after. This is weaker evidence, and the report should say so.

Whatever you do, track a few guardrail metrics too, like trial quality, activation, retention and support load. Otherwise you can win a test and lose a customer.

An audit can’t promise a lift. Its recommendations are hypotheses with different levels of support behind them. If someone shows you a headline conversion number, ask whether it came from one controlled test or a before-and-after comparison, and what else changed at the same time. A money-back guarantee is a commercial term. It isn’t evidence about outcomes.

Seven questions to ask before you buy an audit

  1. Which business question will this answer? “Improve our UX” isn’t one. “Why do trial users stop before connecting their data?” is.
  2. What sits behind each finding? Analytics, recordings, real users, or one expert’s opinion? All of those can be fine, as long as the report says which.
  3. Will findings be labeled as observed fact or hypothesis, with a confidence level? If everything is stated with the same certainty, something is being overstated.
  4. How will issues be sized and ranked? Scoring models like ICE, PIE and PXL are conventions, not validated science. They’re fine as decision aids if the reasoning is visible.
  5. How many people will review it? Given the evaluator effect, one reviewer’s list deserves more caution.
  6. Will the report distinguish UX and conversion issues from problems that require a different response, such as positioning, pricing, acquisition quality, or product strategy?
  7. How will we know a change worked? Ask for a validation method for each recommendation, including what happens if you can’t run an A/B test.
