How to find UX problems in your SaaS product using analytics

Your product analytics can tell you where users abandon a workflow, repeat an action, struggle to find information, or fail to reach value. But the numbers rarely tell you why.

A drop-off on an integration page might indicate confusing UX. It might also mean that the user lacks admin permissions, is waiting for IT, or has no data to connect. Each explanation calls for a different solution.

This guide explains how to use product analytics to identify UX problems in SaaS platforms and dashboards, investigate their causes, and prioritize improvements based on evidence rather than assumptions.

1. What can analytics reveal about your product's UX?

Product analytics records what users do: which pages they visit, which actions they complete, where they stop, how often they return, and how their behavior differs across segments. This makes analytics a powerful starting point for UX diagnosis.

However, behavior is not the same as intent. A user who never opens a feature might not know it exists, might not understand its purpose, might find it difficult to use, or might simply have no reason to use it. Analytics alone cannot reliably distinguish between these explanations.

Three recurring product problems are particularly worth investigating.

Problem 1: Features are built around internal assumptions

Product teams often make decisions based on business priorities, technical possibilities, stakeholder requests, and internal opinions. Over time, the platform accumulates functionality without enough evidence that the features solve important user problems.

A 2019 Pendo analysis of 615 customer subscriptions found that an average of 12% of features accounted for 80% of daily usage volume. This suggests that usage can be heavily concentrated in a relatively small portion of a product's functionality. However, Pendo's sample consisted of its own customers, and the definition of a feature depended on what each customer chose to track. The result should not be treated as a universal SaaS benchmark. Source: Pendo.

More importantly, low usage does not automatically mean a feature is unnecessary. Research by McGrenere and Moore found that familiarity with software functions and actual use were different things. Users could know about functions without using them regularly, and dissatisfaction was often associated with poor implementation, unpredictability, and inconsistency rather than simply having too many features. Source: Graphics Interface.

What to investigate: Is the feature difficult to discover, hard to use, irrelevant to most users, or essential to a smaller but important segment?

Problem 2: Users cannot understand the interface without help

Complex platforms frequently introduce unfamiliar terminology, data structures, configuration options, and workflows. Users may not know what a page is for, what a metric means, or what they are expected to do next.

Teams often respond by adding tutorials, tooltips, documentation, and onboarding tours. These can help, but they do not necessarily solve the underlying problem.

In a Nielsen Norman Group usability test involving 70 users and four mobile apps, participants who read tutorials did not outperform those who skipped them: task success was 91% for the tutorial group and 94% for the group that skipped tutorials. The study involved consumer mobile apps, so it should not be treated as direct proof about enterprise software. It does, however, challenge the assumption that more upfront instruction automatically improves usability. Source: NN/g.

What to investigate: Do users need an explanation, a clearer interface, better terminology, more relevant examples, or a simpler workflow?

Problem 3: New users never experience the product's value

A user signs up, enters the platform, and sees an empty dashboard. Before getting anything useful out of the product, they may need to configure a workspace, connect a data source, request permissions, invite colleagues, and learn unfamiliar concepts.

Every additional step can delay the first meaningful outcome. The user may leave before understanding why the product is worth adopting.

Nielsen Norman Group recommends designing empty states in complex applications to communicate system status, provide learning cues, and give users direct paths to important tasks. Examples include offering demo data or guiding users toward the first setup action. Source: NN/g.

What to investigate: How long does it take users to reach a meaningful outcome, what blocks them, and can they understand the product before completing every setup requirement?

2. Define success before measuring it

Before opening an analytics dashboard, define what a successful user experience means for the task you are investigating.

A common mistake is to measure feature usage instead of the outcome the feature is supposed to support. For example, tracking how often users apply filters is less useful than understanding whether they can identify the information needed to make a decision.

The Goals–Signals–Metrics approach, introduced in research on Google's HEART framework, provides a useful starting point:

For example:

Element Example: identifying at-risk accounts
User goal Find accounts that need attention and decide what to do.
Useful signals Open an account, inspect relevant details, and record or initiate a follow-up action.
Potential metrics Time to identify an account, task completion rate, and percentage of relevant accounts receiving an action.
Guardrails Error rate, incorrect actions, and support requests.

The right metric depends on the job the product performs. A monitoring dashboard may be successful when a user checks it briefly and leaves because nothing needs attention. A decision-support dashboard may require a subsequent action. Treating both as successful only when users interact extensively would produce misleading conclusions.

For workplace software, Nielsen Norman Group's CASTLE framework offers a useful set of dimensions: Cognitive load, Advanced feature usage, Satisfaction, Task efficiency, Learnability, and Errors. Source: NN/g.

3. Analytics methods for finding UX problems

Different analytical methods reveal different kinds of problems. The most useful approach is to combine them around a specific user goal instead of collecting as many metrics as possible.

Funnel analysis: find where progress stops

A funnel tracks the steps users take toward a defined outcome. For example, a B2B platform might track:

The purpose is not simply to identify the step with the largest number of exits. It is to identify unexpected losses relative to the journey, the product's historical baseline, and comparable user segments.

A funnel should represent actual progress toward a goal, not merely a sequence of page views. It should also account for B2B workflows that may take days to complete or require an administrator to perform part of the setup.

Path analysis: identify loops and unexpected journeys

Path analysis shows what users do before and after a particular event. It can reveal repeated navigation between sections, detours, and unexpected routes to a task.

For example, users may repeatedly move between a list page and a detail page because they cannot compare items efficiently. Alternatively, this may be a perfectly normal review workflow. The pattern identifies a question to investigate, not a confirmed UX problem.

Cohort analysis: compare groups over time

Cohorts help establish whether user behavior changes after a product release, an onboarding change, or a change in acquisition strategy. Compare users who started during different periods, using the same definitions of activation and successful usage.

In B2B products, account cohorts may be just as important as individual user cohorts. An employee can be ready to use a platform while the organization is still waiting for an integration or security approval.

Segmentation: discover problems hidden in averages

An overall conversion rate can conceal large differences between user groups. Segment behavior by relevant dimensions such as:

  • User role and permission level.
  • Account size and account age.
  • Acquisition channel and signup route.
  • Plan, device, or experience level.
  • Integration status and availability of real data.

If experienced users complete a workflow successfully but new users repeatedly abandon it, the problem may concern learnability rather than the workflow's underlying functionality. If the problem occurs almost exclusively for non-admin users, the actual barrier may be permissions.

Feature adoption analysis: separate discovery from value

Do not measure feature adoption using a single event such as opening a page. Track the progression from exposure to meaningful use and repeat use.

These stages help distinguish a feature that is difficult to discover from one that users discover but cannot use successfully. They also help reveal features that serve only a specific role or workflow.

Search analytics: find what users cannot locate

Internal search can expose gaps in navigation, information architecture, and terminology. Useful signals include searches with no results, repeated searches using different terms, and searches that do not lead to a result click or successful task.

Search behavior is particularly useful in complex products where users need to locate entities, settings, reports, documentation, or configuration options. Search logs can also reveal the vocabulary users expect, which may differ from the terminology used by the product team.

Error and frustration signals: look beyond the interface

Repeated clicks, failed submissions, duplicate actions, refreshes, and repeated attempts can indicate that the interface is not responding as expected. Session replay can help teams inspect what happened during those sessions.

However, frustration signals have false positives. Rapid clicking may be normal for pagination or other controls. A workflow may also fail because of network latency, an API error, or a service outage. Investigate frontend errors, backend failures, and performance data before concluding that the interface is at fault.

Session replay: inspect what the metrics cannot explain

Session recordings are useful when analytics has already identified a suspicious pattern. Review a targeted sample of sessions and look for evidence related to your hypotheses: missed controls, repeated attempts, navigation loops, error messages, or unexplained pauses.

Replays do not establish how common a problem is. They show what happened in individual sessions, so use them alongside quantitative data and respect privacy requirements when recording user behavior.

Qualitative research: establish why the pattern occurs

Interviews, usability testing, short in-product surveys, and support ticket analysis help explain behavior that analytics cannot interpret on its own.

A practical sequence is to identify an unusual pattern in analytics, inspect a sample of affected sessions, and then speak with users who experienced the problem. This is more targeted than asking users general questions about whether they like the product.

4. Why the same metric can mean different things

One of the most important skills in UX diagnosis is resisting the temptation to jump directly from a metric to a design solution.

Consider the following examples.

Observed behavior Possible explanations What to investigate
High abandonment on an integration page Confusing instructions, missing permissions, waiting for IT, no relevant data, technical failure, or mismatched expectations. Permission events, integration errors, account status, acquisition channel, and interviews with affected users.
Low feature adoption The feature is undiscovered, difficult to understand, hard to use, naturally infrequent, or relevant to a small segment. Exposure-to-use conversion, repeat use, role-based segmentation, and awareness interviews.
High dashboard views but little interaction Users quickly confirm that everything is fine, cannot interpret the data, see no relevant information, or land on the wrong dashboard. The dashboard's purpose, data availability, downstream actions, and users' interpretation of its contents.
Long time to complete a task A difficult workflow, careful analysis, interruptions, multitasking, or slow system performance. Active versus idle time, performance data, task observation, and the distribution of completion times.
More searches or actions per user Higher engagement, more complex work, or repeated attempts to find the right answer. Successful task completion, zero-result searches, retries, and errors per completed task.
Falling activation rate A UX regression, a change in acquisition mix, an instrumentation problem, or a change in the activation definition. Event volumes, tracking changes, release dates, and conversion by acquisition channel and user segment.

When more activity is actually a bad sign

A well-known example from research on online experiments at Microsoft illustrates how misleading engagement metrics can be. A bug in a Bing experiment produced poor search results. Users conducted more queries, while measured revenue per user also increased. The researchers reported increases of more than 10% in distinct queries per user and more than 30% in revenue per user.

More activity looked positive, but users were searching more because their initial results were worse. The experiment demonstrates why teams must understand the meaning of a metric before treating an increase as an improvement. Read the research.

The same principle applies to SaaS platforms. More clicks, longer sessions, repeated searches, and increased feature usage do not necessarily mean that users are getting more value.

Check instrumentation before diagnosing UX

Sudden changes in analytics may indicate tracking problems rather than changes in user behavior. Check instrumentation when you see:

  • A sharp metric change that coincides with a release.
  • A decline limited to one device, browser, or SDK version.
  • Impossible event sequences or duplicated events.
  • A sudden increase in anonymous users or missing account identifiers.
  • A mismatch between analytics events and backend records.

An incorrect event definition can make a healthy workflow appear broken. Validate the data before proposing a redesign.

5. Finding UX problems in dashboards and data-heavy platforms

Dashboards require a more specific diagnostic approach than many consumer applications. Users may come to them to monitor a situation, investigate a problem, compare information, or make a decision. Each purpose implies a different definition of success.

First, identify the job of the dashboard

Monitoring and awareness: Users need to understand whether anything requires attention. A brief visit with few interactions can indicate success.
Operational decision-making: Users need to identify an issue and take a subsequent action, such as assigning a ticket or contacting an account owner.
Strategic analysis: Users need to explore data, compare segments, filter results, investigate trends, and share findings.

Research by Sarikaya and colleagues analyzed 83 dashboards and found that dashboards support a variety of purposes, from awareness to decision-making and communication. The authors also identified recurring challenges related to data literacy and the complexity of dashboard interactions. Read the research.

Measure whether users can interpret the information

A dashboard may display accurate data while failing to help users understand what the data means or what they should do next.

Look for signals such as:

  • Repeated clicks on metric definitions or help icons.
  • Searches for unfamiliar metrics and terminology.
  • Support requests asking how a number is calculated.
  • Repeated filter changes without a clear next action.
  • Frequent exports to spreadsheets for additional analysis.
  • Screenshots used to explain dashboard findings outside the product.

These patterns are not proof of confusion. Exporting data, for example, may be a legitimate part of an analyst's workflow. But they can reveal situations in which users need additional context or capabilities.

Research by Tory and colleagues, based on interviews with 20 analysts and dashboard users, found that people often moved data from dashboards into other tools to manipulate and understand it. This suggests that teams should investigate what users do after viewing a dashboard, not just whether they open it. Read the study.

Track the connection between information and action

If a dashboard is intended to support decisions, instrument the relationship between viewing information and taking the relevant action. For example:

  1. A manager views a report of at-risk accounts.
  2. The manager opens a specific account.
  3. The manager records a follow-up action.

If many users view the report but few proceed to the next step, investigate whether the information is unclear, irrelevant, incomplete, or difficult to act on. Also check whether the action happens outside the platform.

Check whether the dashboard has enough data

An empty dashboard can be a sign of a new account, a missing integration, an incomplete setup, or a legitimate lack of activity. It should not automatically be interpreted as disengagement.

Segment dashboard behavior by data availability, account maturity, and integration status. If users cannot access the information they need, improving chart design will not solve the underlying problem.

Look for information overload, but do not assume it

A crowded dashboard can make it difficult to identify the most important information. However, a high number of widgets or a low interaction rate does not establish cognitive overload.

Combine behavioral evidence with task-based usability testing, interviews, or a workload assessment. Ask users to explain what they notice first, how they interpret the information, and what decision they would make. If they cannot distinguish critical information from background data, investigate hierarchy, labeling, grouping, and the amount of information presented at once.

6. Why empty states and onboarding deserve special attention

The first experience inside a complex platform can determine whether users discover its value or leave before reaching it.

An empty state is not simply a blank screen. It is a moment when the system has little or no user-specific content to display, and the interface must help users understand the situation and move forward.

A useful empty state should answer three questions:

Why is this empty? Explain the current state of the system.
What can I do here? Make the available action clear.
What happens next? Explain the expected outcome or next step.

Depending on the product, the right approach may include sample data, a demo mode, a template, a guided setup, a clear integration action, or an option to request access from an administrator.

The goal is not to add as many onboarding elements as possible. It is to reduce the distance between entering the product and experiencing a meaningful outcome.

If analytics shows that users repeatedly visit a page without completing the next step, investigate whether the interface communicates the purpose of the page and makes the next action obvious. If users already understand what to do but cannot proceed because they lack permissions or data, the solution needs to address that dependency instead.

7. A step-by-step framework for diagnosing UX problems

The following framework combines product analytics, UX research, and experimentation into a repeatable process. It is designed to prevent teams from jumping directly from an unusual metric to a proposed UI fix.

  1. Define the job.Establish whether the product or page is used for monitoring, decision-making, exploration, or another task. Determine whether use is mandatory or voluntary.
  2. Define the user goal.Describe the outcome the user is trying to achieve, not simply the feature they are expected to use.
  3. Map the critical journey.Identify the steps required to reach the outcome, including external dependencies such as IT approval, data imports, and team handoffs.
  4. Validate the instrumentation.Check event definitions, user and account identifiers, event ordering, and consistency with backend records.
  5. Establish a baseline.Measure completion, time between steps, errors, and relevant outcomes. Segment the data by role, account, acquisition channel, and other meaningful dimensions.
  6. Find the unexpected pattern.Look for unusual drop-offs, repeated attempts, navigation loops, errors, and differences between segments.
  7. Write competing hypotheses.List at least three plausible explanations and specify what evidence would support or contradict each one.
  8. Collect discriminating evidence.Start with existing event properties and error logs. Then inspect relevant session recordings, support tickets, and user feedback. Conduct interviews or usability sessions if the cause remains unclear.
  9. State the root cause.Describe the user problem in terms of what users cannot understand, access, find, or accomplish. Do not phrase the diagnosis as a solution.
  10. Prioritize, test, and monitor.Choose an intervention based on its expected impact, affected users, confidence in the diagnosis, and implementation effort. Define the success metric and guardrails before measuring the result.

Example: users abandon the integration step

Imagine that analytics shows 60% of new accounts never view a populated report within 14 days. This is a hypothetical example, not a real product case study.

Observation: Most new accounts fail to reach the first report.

Initial hypotheses:

  • The integration instructions are confusing.
  • Users lack the permissions required to connect the data source.
  • The source contains no relevant data.
  • The integration is failing for technical reasons.

Segmenting the data reveals that the problem is concentrated among self-serve accounts where the person who signed up is not an administrator. Permission-denied events are common among stalled accounts, and interviews confirm that users need to ask IT for help.

Diagnosis: The first-value journey depends on an administrator the initial user cannot easily involve.

Potential intervention: Add a clear request-access or invite-admin flow, show setup status, and consider providing sample data so users can explore the product before the integration is complete.

Validation: Compare the rate of successful integration and first-report viewing across comparable cohorts. Monitor error rates and support contacts to ensure the change does not introduce new problems.

The important distinction is that the solution follows the evidence. If the data had instead revealed repeated integration errors, the priority would be technical reliability rather than onboarding copy.

8. Prioritize and validate improvements

Once the likely cause is understood, decide which problems deserve attention first. A practical prioritization model considers:

Reach: How many users or accounts are affected?
Impact: How seriously does the problem affect their ability to complete an important task?
Confidence: How strongly does the available evidence support the diagnosis?
Effort: What will it take to implement and maintain the intervention?

Frameworks such as RICE and ICE can help structure these decisions, but they are prioritization heuristics rather than scientifically validated predictions of product success. A numerical score should not disguise weak evidence.

Confidence should reflect the quality and diversity of evidence. A suspicious analytics pattern alone offers limited confidence. Analytics combined with error logs, user interviews, and a reproducible usability failure provides a stronger basis for action.

Choose the right validation method

Use a controlled experiment when there is enough traffic to measure a meaningful effect, randomization is feasible, and the intervention can be tested safely. Define a primary metric and guardrail metrics before the experiment begins.

In lower-traffic B2B products, an A/B test may be impractical. Alternatives include usability testing before and after the change, comparisons of similar account cohorts, or a staged rollout with monitoring. Account differences, seasonal workflows, and changes in acquisition mix must be considered when interpreting before-and-after results.

Validation is essential because a plausible solution is not necessarily an effective one. Research on online experimentation at Microsoft reported that only about one-third of well-designed experiments intended to improve a key metric were successful; roughly one-third were flat and one-third were negative. The finding comes from Microsoft's experimentation context, not from a universal sample of SaaS UX changes, but it is a useful reminder that intuition is not enough.

9. How to find UX problems without an expensive analytics stack

A small product team does not need hundreds of tracked events or a dedicated research department to begin diagnosing UX problems. It needs reliable measurements of the most important user journeys.

A practical starting point:

  1. Choose one important user goal and define what successful completion means.
  2. Instrument the 15–30 events needed to understand the journey, rather than tracking everything indiscriminately.
  3. Build a funnel and segment it by the most important user roles and account types.
  4. Check error rates, integration status, and performance around the largest unexpected drop-off.
  5. Review a targeted sample of session recordings, where appropriate and privacy-compliant.
  6. Tag support tickets and customer-success feedback using a manageable set of problem categories.
  7. Speak with a small number of representative users and observe them completing realistic tasks.
  8. Document the diagnosis, evidence, competing explanations, proposed intervention, and success criteria.
  9. Re-measure the outcome after the change and monitor it through a complete natural usage cycle.

Small usability studies are useful for discovering problems, but their results should not be mistaken for precise estimates of how common those problems are across the entire user base. Use production analytics to estimate prevalence and qualitative research to understand the causes.

10. Common mistakes when diagnosing UX with analytics

  • Assuming low usage means low value.A feature may be undiscovered, difficult to use, relevant to a small group, or needed only occasionally.
  • Using engagement as a universal success metric.More activity can mean that users are struggling. Less activity can mean that a task is easy or that a monitoring dashboard has done its job.
  • Using one blended conversion rate.Differences in roles, permissions, account maturity, and acquisition channel can hide the real problem.
  • Redesigning before checking technical constraints.Performance problems, API errors, and missing permissions can produce behavioral patterns that resemble poor usability.
  • Adding instructions before simplifying the experience.Tutorials cannot compensate for every confusing workflow or unclear product concept.
  • Confusing correlation with causation.An early action associated with retention may be a marker of user motivation rather than the reason users stay.
  • Measuring feature activity instead of user outcomes.A feature is valuable when it helps users accomplish something important, not merely because they interact with it.

Frequently asked questions

How do you identify UX problems using product analytics?

Start with a defined user goal, map the steps required to achieve it, and use funnels, path analysis, segmentation, error data, and session recordings to locate unexpected behavior. Then investigate the cause using user feedback, interviews, support data, or usability testing.

How can I tell whether a feature is unused or undiscovered?

Track whether users encounter the feature's entry point, whether they try its core action, and whether they return to it when relevant. Combine these events with awareness questions and role-based analysis. Low usage alone cannot distinguish an undiscovered feature from an unnecessary one.

What is activation in a SaaS product?

Activation is the point at which a user or account reaches a defined early outcome that indicates meaningful product value. The event should reflect the product's purpose and be checked against later successful usage or retention. Signing up or opening a dashboard is not necessarily activation.

What is time to value?

Time to value measures how long it takes a user or account to experience a meaningful outcome. Define both the starting point and the value event carefully, and account for external dependencies such as integrations, permissions, and team approvals.

Which UX metrics matter most for B2B dashboards?

The most useful metrics depend on the dashboard's job. They may include task completion, time to a decision, error rates, successful downstream actions, learnability, and feature adoption within relevant user segments. A short visit or low interaction rate is not inherently bad.

Conclusion: Diagnose the problem before designing the solution

Analytics is most useful when it helps teams ask better questions. A drop-off, a low adoption rate, or a repeated action is a signal to investigate, not a diagnosis in itself.

The strongest approach combines a clear definition of user success, reliable instrumentation, segmented behavioral data, competing hypotheses, and qualitative evidence. It also accounts for technical reliability, permissions, organizational workflows, and the specific job the product is supposed to perform.

Instead of asking, "Which screen should we redesign?", start with: "What are users trying to accomplish, where does progress break down, and what evidence would explain why?"

That shift helps teams avoid adding features or instructions to solve the wrong problem. It turns UX improvement from a sequence of assumptions into a process of diagnosis, prioritization, and validation.
